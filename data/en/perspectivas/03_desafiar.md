## When the industry confused friction with profitability

In the early years of the internet, many search engines were not only search engines. They were portals. They had news, banners, quick links, weather, finance, email, directories, promotions and a growing number of stimuli designed to keep the user inside the page.

The logic seemed reasonable: if the user visits only a few times, each visit must be monetized as much as possible. If each search is a scarce opportunity, then each screen should load more advertising, more links, more commercial agreements and more sellable inventory.

The industry started from an almost invisible axiom:

> The user has a relatively fixed set of search needs.  
> Therefore, the business consists of extracting as much value as possible from each one.

Under that view, the equation was simple: more advertising per search, more impressions, more revenue. Even if the experience was slower. Even if the page was overloaded. Even if the user had to cross more noise to reach what they wanted.

Google challenged that axiom with a different question:

**What if search demand was not fixed?**

What if, instead of monetizing each search aggressively, the product had to become so fast, clean and useful that the user searched many more times?

## The uncomfortable thesis: sacrifice P to multiply Q

In economic terms, the dilemma can be summarized as a tension between unit price and quantity.

If each search generates high unit revenue but the user searches little, the business can look profitable. But that profitability may be built on an artificial restriction: a poor experience.

Google bet on the opposite thesis:

**lower monetization per interaction to multiply the number of interactions**.

The question was not only how much money one search could produce. The question was how many searches a radically better experience could generate.

In simple form:

```text
Revenue = P x Q
```

Where:

- **P** is average monetization per search;
- **Q** is the number of searches performed.

The dominant hypothesis said: defend **P**.  
Google's hypothesis said: expand **Q**.

Even if **P** falls to 3%, if **Q** multiplies by 1,000, the total business can become much larger. The key is understanding whether demand is truly fixed or whether it is being suppressed by product friction.

That was the strategic point: Google was not only competing against other search engines. It was competing against the idea that search was an occasional, limited and sufficiently annoying action that should be monetized intensely each time it happened.

## Search as the product, not as an advertising excuse

The major difference was that Google treated search as the main product, not as an excuse to show advertising.

The clean homepage, response speed and relevance of results conveyed a very clear promise: come in, search and keep moving.

That could seem counterintuitive. If the user left quickly, was an opportunity for monetization being lost? If the page had less advertising, would revenue fall? If the search engine solved the problem too well, would it reduce exposure time?

In reality, the opposite happened. By reducing friction, Google increased usage frequency. Search stopped being an occasional tool and became an everyday reflex.

Search for an address.  
Search for a word.  
Search for a product.  
Search for a small doubt.  
Search for a company.  
Search for a news item.  
Search for anything.

Speed changed behavior.

The user did not perform the same number of searches in less time. The user performed many more searches because the mental cost of searching fell almost to zero.

## The best advertising was not looking like advertising

The other break was in advertising.

While other portals overloaded the experience with banners and poorly integrated commercial agreements, Google built a model where advertising appeared closer to the user's intent.

It was not only about showing ads. It was about showing ads linked to a specific search, at the moment the user expressed a need.

That changed the economic quality of advertising.

For the advertiser, the value was not interrupting someone while they browsed. It was appearing when someone was already looking for something. For the user, advertising could become less annoying because, in some cases, it was part of the path toward a solution.

Google did not eliminate monetization. It made monetization less invasive, more contextual and more scalable.

And in doing so, it turned user trust into the central asset of the business.

## The cost of challenging the axiom

This type of strategy always creates internal and external resistance.

From a traditional financial spreadsheet, the risk was obvious. Less advertising per page could mean less revenue per session. An experience that was too fast could reduce time spent. A clean interface could look like underused commercial inventory.

For investors and managers, the question was uncomfortable:

**why give up visible revenue today for a volume hypothesis tomorrow?**

The problem is that many companies do not die because they lack data. They die because they interpret data inside the wrong axiom.

If the number of searches is assumed to be fixed, Google seemed to be leaving money on the table.

But if the number of searches is understood to be elastic to speed, relevance and trust, then the company was not giving up revenue. It was buying future behavior.

It was investing in habit.

## When competitors get trapped in their own model

The incumbents' reaction was not simple.

Many competitors had advertising contracts, commercial commitments and revenue structures designed around the old model. They had promised visibility, traffic, placements, banners, impressions and highlighted presence. Their business was built on capturing value in each visit, not on making friction disappear.

So if they wanted to imitate Google, they had to break with their own advertisers, reduce visible commercial inventory, redesign their portals and accept an initial drop in monetization.

But if they did not do it, the user experience kept deteriorating against a faster and more focused competitor.

That is one of the most powerful effects of challenging an axiom correctly: **innovation does not only improve your product; it also makes your competitors' business model uncomfortable**.

Google became the internet user's best friend because it took the user's side at the right moment. And once the user internalized that searching on Google was simpler, faster and more reliable, looking elsewhere started to carry a cost.

Habit became competitive advantage.

## The privileged place in the user's mind

Some strategic positions are not won with technology alone. They are won through repetition, trust and low cognitive cost.

Google achieved a privileged mental position: when someone needed to know something, they did not think about "using a search engine". They thought about "googling".

That leap is enormous.

A brand becomes a verb when it stops being an option and becomes a behavior. And when a behavior becomes automatic, the competition is no longer fighting only against a company. It is fighting against an installed habit.

That is why Google's advantage was so persistent. It was not only a better algorithm, a clean interface or a more efficient advertising model. It was the combination of all those elements in an experience that reduced friction and multiplied use.

Google understood that in some markets, **the unit of analysis is not the transaction, but frequency**.

## But lowering P does not always multiply Q

The lesson is not that lowering price, margin or monetization always works.

That is a bad reading of the Google case.

The real lesson is more precise: lowering **P** works when there is sufficiently elastic latent demand, when the product improves with adoption and when additional volume does not destroy unit economics.

If those conditions do not exist, lowering P only accelerates the problem.

### MoviePass: when Q grows and destroys the business

MoviePass is one of the clearest counterexamples. The company offered an almost unlimited cinema plan for a very low monthly price. Volume grew, the product became attractive to consumers and adoption increased rapidly.

But there was a structural problem: each additional use generated a relevant real cost. The company did not have low enough marginal economics or enough power over cinemas to capture the value it was creating. In practice, the more the best customers used the product, the more pressure the cash position suffered.

The result was a brutal paradox: **growth made the economics of the business worse**.

Lowering P did multiply Q. But Q was not profitable. It was a cash-burning machine.

### J.C. Penney: when the customer did not want efficiency, but ritual

Another different case was J.C. Penney. The company tried to replace discounts, coupons and promotions with simpler and more transparent pricing. In theory, it was a rational proposal: less promotional manipulation, more clarity and better everyday prices.

But the customer did not respond as the company expected.

For part of its consumer base, the discount was not only a price reduction. It was part of the shopping experience. The coupon, the offer and the feeling of having found an opportunity worked as a commercial ritual.

The company reduced complexity, but it also removed a psychological component that its customers valued.

The lesson there is different: **not every friction is perceived as friction by the customer**. Sometimes what the company considers inefficient is part of perceived value.

### Quibi: when price does not solve a nonexistent need

Quibi also shows the limit of the strategy. The platform tried to sell short premium content for mobile consumption. The price was not necessarily prohibitive, but the main problem was elsewhere: the need was not clear enough.

The consumer already had free or better-positioned alternatives for short video, such as YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, and stronger alternatives for premium entertainment, such as Netflix or Disney+.

In that case, lowering P could not multiply Q sustainably because the problem was not only price. It was value proposition, consumption context and habit.

When the need is already satisfied by other products, a low price can reduce the trial barrier, but it does not necessarily create recurrence.

## The right question before lowering margin

Before sacrificing monetization, margin or unit price, a company should ask itself a fundamental question:

**Am I releasing suppressed demand, or simply selling something cheaper that the market already understands?**

The difference is enormous.

Google lowered friction and expanded use because the need to search for information was enormous, growing and still poorly served. In addition, each additional search had low marginal cost and fed a more valuable advertising system.

MoviePass, by contrast, lowered the price of an experience with high marginal cost.  
J.C. Penney simplified prices without understanding the customer's emotional relationship with promotion.  
Quibi offered a paid alternative for a need that other products already solved better.

In one case, lowering P opened a market.  
In the others, lowering P exposed a weakness.

## The strategic lesson

The Google case does not teach that companies should always monetize less.

It teaches that some industry axioms hide enormous opportunities.

When everyone believes demand is fixed, the company that discovers demand is expandable can change the size of the market. When everyone optimizes monetization per transaction, the company that optimizes frequency can build a much deeper advantage. When everyone tries to capture value in each interaction, the company that reduces friction can win the whole relationship.

But that bet requires one condition: the additional volume must be economically healthy.

Because **P x Q** is not a magic formula. It is a reality test.

If Q grows with good unit economics, the strategy can create a giant.  
If Q grows by destroying margin, it only accelerates the fall.  
If Q does not grow, the company simply gave value away.

Google won because it understood that search was not a scarce occasion to monetize.

It was a latent habit waiting for an experience good enough to multiply.

And when a company turns a latent need into an everyday habit, it stops competing only for revenue.

It starts competing for a place in the mind.
