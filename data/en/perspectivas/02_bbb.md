## When competing affordably does not mean destroying value

In highly competitive industries such as fashion retail, there is a permanent tension between price, quality and profitability. The customer wants to pay less. The supplier wants to be paid fairly. The employee expects a reward. And the company builds a value chain where it must then share, distribute and defend its margin.

In that context, the good, attractive and affordable strategy often looks like a contradiction, even a utopia. How can a company offer appealing products, reasonable quality and accessible prices without destroying its own profitability?

The scarcest and most important resource for solving that equation is the discipline to define a reasonable average margin and sustain it over time.

Not maximizing the margin on every transaction. Not capturing all possible surplus every season. Not transferring every tension to the supplier, the employee or the customer. Instead, the company defines an expected average return, for example between 7% and 14%, and builds the whole commercial system around that rule.

## Profitability as an anchor

In many retail businesses, margin is managed reactively. If demand is high, prices go up. If there is excess inventory, products are discounted. If the supplier is weak, negotiation becomes harder. If cash is tight, headcount is reduced. If the competition lowers prices, the response is another promotion.

The result is an unstable system. Nobody knows exactly what to expect. The people involved start diversifying or reducing dependence, which often damages the quality of the relationship and the product.

The customer learns to wait for discounts. The supplier prices in uncertainty. The employee works under constant commercial pressure. And the company lives on a roller coaster of cash, stock and margin. In a way, it becomes a prisoner's dilemma where fear and uncertainty push the system away from the optimum.

An average-margin strategy works differently. The company defines the margin it needs to operate, reinvest and sustain the business. Then it aligns pricing, purchasing, promotions, inventory and supplier relationships around that objective.

## Transparency as a strategic asset

Once the target profitability is clear, relationships become simpler. Not because conflict disappears, but because the game is no longer ambiguous.

The supplier knows it will not be squeezed every time the company has bargaining power. That trust is difficult to create, but a B2B relationship where both parties know the other is seeking a genuinely mutually beneficial agreement tends to favor long-term ties. A loyal, predictable and quality counterparty becomes more valuable than occasional opportunities.

Then there is the customer. The customer understands that the price does not depend on an artificial promotion or a permanent clearance event. What is displayed is what it costs now and what it will likely cost later. The purchase decision becomes simpler because it is about the product and the need, not timing.

The employee, in turn, works in an organization with more stable rules. Within that fixed profitability logic, there can be incentives to retain people who understand the system and know where their value lies.

In a business such as fashion retail, this can be especially powerful. The industry often operates with high volatility: seasons, trends, demand shifts, inventory pressure, markdowns, cost inflation and extreme price sensitivity.

Against that backdrop, disciplined average-margin policy introduces predictability. It builds an architecture where price becomes the consequence of efficiency, rotation, trust and value proposition. The company can project the future more easily.

## Being affordable requires more discipline than selling expensive

There is a mistaken idea that competing on affordability is easy. In reality, competing affordably and profitably is extremely difficult.

Selling expensive can hide inefficiencies for a while. A high margin tolerates purchasing mistakes, poor planning, hidden costs, low rotation or imprecise pricing decisions. But it also creates the risk that, once the effect fades, the consumer migrates toward another value proposition. The business becomes dependent on the customer.

When a company decides to operate with a limited average margin, the margin for error narrows. This is especially true because trust-based relationships take years to build and seconds to destroy.

> "It takes 20 years to build a reputation and five minutes to ruin it."
>
> Warren Buffett

Choosing SKUs matters. The customer offer must be right. Buying quality matters. Avoiding oversized inventory matters. Controlling logistics, financial and operating costs matters. Understanding which products attract traffic, which products generate real profitability and how to balance both matters.

The affordable value strategy is not simple. It is a strategy of high precision, long-term thinking and deep operating discipline.

## The natural barrier: few want to play such a long game

The competitive advantage appears because this model is not easy to copy.

Many incumbents can lower prices for a season. They can launch aggressive promotions. They can sacrifice margin for market share. They can pressure suppliers for better terms. They can liquidate inventory to generate cash.

But for how long can they really sustain it? Are investors willing to burn that much cash for an uncertain result? And above all, why give up short-term benefits that may offer even higher returns?

When demand allows prices to rise, the company must hold back. That accumulated capital, credibility, is what sets it apart. When the supplier is weak, the company must avoid capturing all bargaining power. When the customer is willing to pay more, the company must decide whether to maximize margin today or sustain trust tomorrow. When the market becomes disorderly, it must resist the temptation to use promotion as its only response.

At some point, the commercial relationship becomes almost a stable passive flow of income. And the customer finds peace of mind in an environment that is otherwise stressful.

That discipline creates a natural barrier to entry.

Not because there is a patent, a secret technology or an impossible contract. Because the model requires coherence, patience and a business culture that prioritizes the durability of the system over the immediate maximization of each transaction.

## Average margin as an implicit contract

When a company sustains a reasonable average margin for a long time, something interesting happens: an implicit contract starts to form among all participants.

With suppliers, the relationship stops being purely transactional. There is more room to plan, share risks, improve terms, sustain quality and cross difficult cycles without breaking the relationship. Forms of collaboration can also appear without integration: joint investments, shared planning and coordinated capability development.

With employees, the stability of the model makes it easier to build processes, incentives and learning. The organization is not permanently reacting to commercial or financial urgency.

With customers, the value proposition becomes credible. Price stops depending on the clearance event and becomes part of the brand identity. You get what you pay for without having to decode the game.

A form of economic solidarity can even appear.

Today I absorb part of the shock. Tomorrow you absorb it. At another moment we adjust together. Not from naivety, but from a practical understanding: in long value chains, nobody wins sustainably if everyone tries to capture the maximum possible value at every point of the cycle.

The cost of switching is often high as well. Together, those two forces make suppliers, employees and customers more likely to remain part of an established, reliable and predictable value chain.

## The strategic lesson

The good, attractive and affordable strategy is not about selling cheap. Selling cheap is the consequence of a business model.

It means designing a system where price, margin, suppliers, employees and customers are aligned around two rules: transparency and mutual trust.

Sometimes the strongest business is not the one that earns the most on each sale.

In highly competitive industries, the real advantage is not in a single link. It is in building a system that works with precision.

And in having patience.

Another scarce good.
