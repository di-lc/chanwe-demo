## When the supermarket stops being the center of the business

For decades, the hypermarket was one of the great promises of modern consumption. Large surfaces, parking, variety, family trips, food courts and the idea of solving almost everything in a single visit. In many inland Argentine cities, that model did more than work; it organized commercial life around a point of attraction.

But consumption habits changed. And when the consumer changes, the whole value chain reorganizes. Competitive advantages can become weaknesses, and the pride of believing they are still valid can lead to a slow decline.

Grupo Libertad is an interesting case because it shows a business decision that can be summarized in an uncomfortable but unavoidable phrase: **adapt or disappear**. The company, historically associated with Libertad hypermarkets, reduced its direct exposure to the traditional supermarket business and reinforced another asset: its shopping promenades.

In other words, it moved from competing mainly on the shelf to capturing value as a real-estate operator, owner and manager of commercial spaces. At first it may seem extreme, but in the supermarket world a core part of success lies in analyzing exploitable value correctly, estimating post-construction potential and developing the commercial complex.

## The strategic thesis: less shelf, more platform

The difference matters. A supermarket competes every day against:

- aggressive prices
- permanent promotions
- labor and logistics costs
- narrow margins
- inflation and lower consumption
- online platforms
- wholesalers
- proximity stores
- Chinese self-service formats
- imported products

It is an intense, volatile and operationally heavy business. Above all, it is a business of tight margins.

A commercial promenade can work differently. The owner of the space does not depend only on selling rice, milk or detergent. The business becomes:

- attracting traffic
- selecting good tenants
- combining categories
- managing the commercial experience
- collecting rent
- turning the property into a consumption ecosystem

In strategic terms, Libertad seems to have read a clear signal: the hypermarket is no longer necessarily the center of the shopping experience. The company also understood that its strengths were not only in retail operations, but in real estate. What may look like a 180-degree turn is really a shift in focus: from 80/20 to 20/80.

## The cultural shift in consumption habits

When competition becomes hostile and the consumer fragments, the robustness of each strategy becomes clearer. Today, consumers buy online, compare prices, use promotions, split their ticket across channels and feel less loyalty toward a single large format.

The supermarket visit is no longer, for many people, a trip in itself. The consumer now operates with a more flexible logic:

- buying nearby when convenience matters
- buying online for assortment or comfort
- buying wholesale when price comes first
- comparing promotions before deciding
- splitting spending across several formats
- reducing loyalty to a single chain

Faced with that context, the traditional hypermarket loses centrality. It does not disappear, but it stops being the dominant format for every consumption occasion.

Pressure from both consumers and suppliers, with more distribution channels and greater leverage across the value chain, has a direct impact on margins.

Then the company changes the question. Grupo Libertad noticed that the supermarket generated a network effect on neighboring businesses in the promenade, and that it had a talent for identifying locations with strong consumer traffic and for creating a physical shopping experience. Instead of spilling value without capturing it while fighting other supermarkets and sales channels, it focused elsewhere.

## The asset does not disappear: the monetization model changes

Many companies do not have bad assets. They use valuable assets under an old logic. A well-located property, with parking, circulation and brand recognition, can be worth more as a commercial center than as an oversized hypermarket.

The asset remains valuable. What changes is the model used to monetize it. In this case, the logic seems to shift from:

- selling products
- managing inventory
- competing on price
- absorbing operating costs
- sustaining large sales surfaces

Toward a different logic:

- managing square meters
- capturing real-estate income
- administering traffic
- operating a commercial mix
- converting a location into a consumption platform

That transition redefines how the business looks at retail and tries to capture value.

## From supermarket operator to ecosystem owner

Libertad's decision can be read as a migration from an operating-margin business toward a business of rent, location and traffic management.

Instead of carrying all the complexity of supermarket operations, the company keeps control of the commercial environment and allows a specialized player to operate the anchor store.

It is a way of saying:

> I do not need to win every battle on the shelf if I can be the conductor of the terrain where several commercial battles take place.

The business stops depending only on how much the supermarket sells and starts depending on the ability to transform a physical location into an attractive commercial node.

## The lesson for other companies

The lesson is powerful.

Adapting drastically can be less drastic than it seems when a company looks honestly at its true advantages and strengths.

Sometimes adapting means:

- shrinking in one dimension to strengthen another
- reducing exposure to gain focus
- selling an operation while keeping the platform
- trading volume for profitability
- trading operational identity for strategic resilience
- reinforcing the assets where negotiation power still exists

It also requires humility. Many companies cling to their historical definition:

- "we are a supermarket"
- "we are a store"
- "we are a factory"
- "we are a bank"

But the companies that survive usually ask a deeper question:

> What do we truly know how to do better than others?
