## From the "Coca-Cola moment" to lost relevance

It is curious how, in many corporate hallways, someone still appears who believes that reading financial statements means understanding the business better than the people who built it. *"The numbers don't lie"* is one of the most dangerous phrases in modern management. Numbers do not lie, but they also do not tell the whole truth.

Nike reached the top after decades of product, athletes, distribution, culture and desire. It was not just a company that sold sneakers. It was a machine of meaning: performance, identity, competition, style, belonging.

The problem began when part of that machine started to be seen through a single lens: the financial one.

A balance sheet can support a story or reveal inconsistencies. But the architecture that creates value almost never fits entirely inside a spreadsheet. What is the value proposition? Where does competitive advantage live? Which relationships sustain it? Which consumer habits do not appear in a dashboard? Which part of the business looks like a cost, but is actually infrastructure?

Put simply: a company may spend 30% more than the sector average on salaries. That can be terrible hiring management or a brilliant human-capital strategy. The number is the same. The meaning is not.

Something similar happened at Nike with its sales channels.

Wholesale began to be read as margin given away, operational complexity and loss of proprietary data. In other words: as cost.

But it was not only that.

It was part of the strategic machinery.

In 2020, John Donahoe became Nike's CEO. He came from Bain & Company, eBay and ServiceNow: three worlds where efficiency, data, platforms and process optimization matter enormously. He did not come, however, from sports fashion, product design, sneaker culture or the obsession with each sport.

The financial diagnosis looked elegant: if Nike sold more directly to consumers, it would capture more margin, more data and more control.

The thesis was simple.

> Nike didn't fail because Direct-to-Consumer was wrong.
> Nike failed because finance ended up designing strategy.

DTC was not the problem.

The problem was confusing channel with strategy, margin with value and proprietary data with market intelligence.

## Strategy is art, passion and data

The common reading says Nike simply overestimated DTC.

That is true, but incomplete.

The recipe Donahoe tried to accelerate made sense on paper:

- eliminate wholesale margins;
- own direct customer data;
- improve gross margin;
- reduce channel complexity;
- increase ROIC, or return on invested capital.

All perfectly rational.

Inside a spreadsheet.

The error was assuming that selling sneakers, sportswear and cultural identity works like selling software, marketplaces or digital services. In those businesses, disintermediation can increase control without destroying too much context. In fashion, sport and culture, context is part of the product.

What Nike underestimated was the invisible asset wholesale gave it.

Distributors were not just points of sale. They were:

- **free marketing:** premium windows in malls, high streets and specialty stores;
- **local recommendation:** salespeople guiding consumers at the moment of decision;
- **market intelligence:** signals about what people buy when they compare brands side by side;
- **shelf space:** physical presence in the consumer's field of vision;
- **geographic capillarity:** reach into places where a Nike-owned store would never be profitable;
- **experimentation:** constant exposure to audiences that had not yet decided to buy Nike.

For decades, the swoosh generated relevance, distinction, identity and aspiration. Its products carried a competitive electricity that was hard to replicate. But that electricity did not live only in the product. It also lived in distribution.

Nike was everywhere. And, more importantly, it was next to its competitors.

On the same wall. On the same shelf. In the same moment of comparison.

That physical proximity was part of the cultural battle.

Thousands of multi-brand stores acted as distributed marketing. Nike reduced that presence because it looked like a cost. It was actually strategic infrastructure.

Some explain the deterioration through trend shifts Nike failed to read. There is some truth in that. But the uncomfortable question is different: did trends change on their own, or did Nike, by retreating, help consumers discover alternatives that had previously been in the background?

Nature hates empty spaces.

Business too.

## Cutting costs is fine; cutting any cost is not

Cost reduction creates value only when the eliminated asset is non-strategic.

If you remove strategic infrastructure, someone else will occupy that space.

While Nike reduced its presence in multi-brand stores, **On, Hoka, New Balance, Brooks** and others gained space. Retailers still needed premium sneakers to fill their walls. The chair Nike left was not removed from the room.

Someone sat in it.

The paradox is that the decision designed to increase margin and capture data ended up damaging other sources of value. Nike gained a more direct relationship with some customers, but lost market signals that came from commercial partners. It reduced intermediaries, but also reduced presence. It improved control over certain touchpoints, but weakened the physical comparison where it had won for years.

Not all costs are equal.

Some costs are expense.

Some costs are friction.

And some costs are disguised investment.

At Nike, part of the margin ceded to wholesalers was also distributed marketing budget, commercial intelligence, defense of physical space and a barrier against competition. Had the company read it that way, the migration to DTC would have been more gradual, more selective and less dogmatic.

## Technocratic arrogance

Nike cut ties or reduced inventory with long-standing distributors such as Foot Locker, DSW and Macy's. The implicit assumption was strong: if consumers could not find Nike in those places, they would download the app, enter the digital ecosystem and buy direct.

But real purchasing behavior is rarely that obedient.

People buy through desire, yes. But also through convenience, comparison, impulse, recommendation, availability and context. Often, they do not want to enter a brand's closed world. They want to see options. Touch them. Try them on. Compare them.

Nike always took risks. That was not the problem.

The problem is that this time it was not improving the secret sauce. It was changing the pipes of a system that was not broken.

The *Consumer Direct Acceleration* strategy also pushed an internal reorganization. Nike moved away from part of its historic sport- and category-based logic and prioritized more centralized structures, oriented around data, the consumer and commercial efficiency. In theory, that could simplify the operation. In practice, it weakened a fundamental sensitivity: that of teams who understood the specific culture of each sport.

Running is not basketball.

Basketball is not football.

Football is not training.

A sports brand cannot read all of those communities as interchangeable segments in a matrix.

---

## The unhappy ending of a textbook management story

By leaving certain shelves, Nike indirectly financed the growth of its competitors. It left them the most valuable asset in physical retail: visibility at the moment of decision.

But the deepest damage was the innovation crisis. Obsessed with digital margins, efficiency and the optimization of existing models, Nike leaned too heavily on its classics: Air Force 1, Dunk, Air Jordan 1 and other retro silhouettes. Releasing new colorways works for a while. It does not replace creating the future.

Consumers get bored when novelty feels like an inventory variation.

The story of Nike in the Donahoe era does not prove that DTC is bad. It proves something more useful: a great brand cannot be managed like a software company. A sports brand lives from product, culture, athletes, distribution, community, timing and desire.

Finance optimizes existing systems.

Strategy designs future systems.

Confusing both disciplines can produce one of the most dangerous forms of deterioration: a beautifully optimized decline.
