## When the business looks solid, but cash does not follow

Some companies fail because the product does not work. Others fail because they lose relevance. Others because they never found profitability.

But there is a more uncomfortable kind of failure: companies with **real businesses, real customers, known brands and an apparently solid commercial position** that still cannot survive a cash crisis. It is normal to understand a solvency crisis as excess debt or lack of profitability. But a cash crisis turning into a solvency crisis almost sounds like a lie in modern finance.

That was the case of **Conviviality plc**.

Conviviality was not an experimental startup or a company without demand. It was a British beverage retail and distribution group with recognized brands such as **Bargain Booze**, **Wine Rack**, **Matthew Clark** and **Bibendum**. It operated both in retail, with beverage and convenience stores, and in wholesale distribution to pubs, restaurants, hotels and other *on-trade* customers.

In other words: it was not selling smoke. It sold alcohol, beverages, convenience and distribution. And it did it well. A tangible, frequent business, with recurring demand and visible brands. It even had solid suppliers, loyal customers and, above all, formidable commercial reach.

In some businesses, **cash is not a consequence of the business**: it is the infrastructure that allows the business to exist. Put differently, in a seasonal business with margins that are not especially generous, *cash is king*.

## What Conviviality sold

Conviviality operated in two connected worlds.

On one side, it had the retail business, with brands such as **Bargain Booze** and **Wine Rack**. Bargain Booze was a British chain of beverage and convenience stores, with a strong franchise presence. Its proposition was simple: availability, price, proximity and recurring purchases.

On the other side, it had the distribution business, especially through **Matthew Clark** and **Bibendum**. That arm supplied thousands of hospitality customers: pubs, bars, hotels, restaurants and businesses that needed beverages delivered reliably and continuously.

The strategic logic was attractive. Conviviality could place itself at the center of a fragmented value chain:

- buying from beverage producers and brands;
- distributing to commercial customers;
- operating stores and franchises;
- capturing demand information;
- gaining scale in purchasing, logistics and portfolio;
- connecting manufacturers with points of consumption.

In theory, it was a good place to be. It was a commercial platform inside the beverage ecosystem, full of information and bargaining power.

## The appeal of the position

The business had a clear thesis: in a high-volume industry with a long chain, meaning narrow margins, multiple suppliers and thousands of final customers, only the intermediary that achieves scale, reach and commercial relationships can capture value.

Consumers bought drinks.  
Bars needed supply.  
Restaurants needed reliable providers.  
Brands needed distribution.  
Franchisees needed assortment, price and support.

The company was positioned in an apparently robust place: between **recurring demand** and **the operational complexity of serving it**. Two attractive value propositions, joined under the same roof.

That type of business can look safe when it is well positioned: people consume regularly, bars need frequent supply and stores need inventory rotation.

But that apparent stability hides a fragility: **working capital**.

## The business was solid until it had to finance itself

Distribution and retail have a fundamental characteristic: before selling, the company must buy; before collecting, it must finance; before generating margin, it must sustain inventory, suppliers, logistics, taxes and payroll.

Cash engineering is central. All that competitive power required meticulous financial scheduling. That is the heart of the Conviviality case.

The company could have revenue, scale and valuable commercial assets. But its operation depended on several pieces continuing to work at the same time, almost like a quartz watch: if one link failed, the system could collapse.

- suppliers willing to sell on normal terms;
- credit insurers willing to cover commercial risk;
- banks willing to maintain credit lines;
- shareholders willing to contribute capital if needed;
- reliable cash forecasts;
- customers paying within expected terms;
- inventory available so the operation did not break.

Like every weakness, while everything works, it looks administrative. When one piece breaks, it becomes **the elephant in the room**: how did nobody see it coming if it was so obvious?

## The elephant in the room

Even worse, it is the kind of problem that slowly kills the frog in the pot. It was a gradual accumulation of tensions.

PwC, in its administration reports, noted that the group had already been experiencing **short-term cash difficulties**, linked to investments in the retail business, system implementation, integration costs and working-capital pressures. Those issues were compounded by weaknesses in cash forecasting. That point was the biggest clue of all.

Many liquidity crises do not begin when money is missing. They begin earlier, when the company loses a reliable view of when cash will be missing and how much will be missing. Major decisions are made around resource allocation, use of credit lines and financial timing.

A poor cash forecast is not a minor technical error. It is driving with a fogged windshield. The company can keep moving, but it no longer really knows how fast the curve is approaching.

## The profit warning that changed the conversation

On March 8, 2018, Conviviality announced that it expected to be approximately **20% below profit expectations** for the year ending April 30, 2018. That announcement triggered a partial withdrawal of credit insurance which, to executives, should not have been a major problem. On the surface, it added "some more pressure" to the business's cash flow.

But the fatal mistake was underestimating the effect that the withdrawal of insurance could have. It is a normal and recurring mistake even among leading companies: believing they are "too big to fail", with the arrogance of thinking the system needs them more than it actually does.

In a distribution company, credit insurance is not a peripheral detail. It is part of the trust system that allows suppliers to sell, commercial terms to remain in place and the operation to keep breathing.

When credit insurance is reduced, suppliers start looking differently.  
When suppliers look differently, commercial terms change.  
When commercial terms change, cash tightens.  
When cash tightens, the market becomes afraid.  
When the market becomes afraid, everyone tries to protect themselves first.

That is where the vicious cycle begins.

Liquidity does not disappear only because there is less money. It disappears because every actor starts demanding protection at the same time. Almost like a bank run: if trust breaks, everyone asks for their money at once.

## The final blow: a tax bill that was not on the map

Days later, on March 14, 2018, the company announced the identification of an approximately **GBP 30 million** tax liability with HMRC, due on March 29, that had not been included in the short-term cash forecast. This created an immediate and unexpected financing need.

That is the moment when the problem stops being uncomfortable and becomes existential.

A company can absorb bad news if it has cash. Cash is not only lubricant for the business: it is both a catcher of opportunities and a shock absorber for surprises. It can absorb mistakes if it has time and can even renegotiate if it preserves trust.

But Conviviality lost all four in such a short period that every piece of news became magnified.

The tax liability was a signal: if a bill of that size was not properly included in the forecast, what else could be wrong? People often say that one thing is chance, two are coincidence, but three are a pattern.

The crisis stopped being about liquidity. It became a solvency crisis.

## When everyone runs toward the exit

After the announcement, the company tried to secure emergency financing. There was discussion of an approximately **GBP 125 million** equity raise to stabilize the group. But the transaction did not succeed, and Conviviality entered administration. A few months earlier, that kind of transaction would have been a walk in the park for the company.

Imagine that, if investors had believed the problem was only temporary, capital could have appeared. If banks, suppliers, insurers and shareholders had trusted management, a bridge could have been built. After all, Conviviality's numbers had not changed that much: it would have been a profitability shock and nothing more.

## From liquidity crisis to solvency crisis

In theory, Conviviality still had valuable assets.

In fact, those assets did not disappear. They were sold. The retail business, including **Bargain Booze** and **Wine Rack**, was sold to Bestway for approximately **GBP 7.25 million**. The wholesale arm, including **Matthew Clark** and **Bibendum**, was acquired by C&C Group, a transaction that reportedly saved around 2,000 jobs at the time.

That reinforces the thesis.

- The business was not worth zero.  
- The brands were not irrelevant.  
- The customers had not disappeared.  
- The operation had salvageable parts.

A liquidity crisis asks:

**can you pay now?**

A solvency crisis asks:

**is your whole structure still viable after trust has been lost?**

In Conviviality, the lack of cash accelerated forced sales, loss of control, administration and value destruction for creditors and shareholders. Later PwC reports indicated that secured creditors had exposure of approximately **GBP 169 million** and would suffer a significant shortfall in recoveries.

Liquidity was not only missing. It became a self-fulfilling prophecy among shareholders and creditors alike: the lack of liquidity changed the value of everything else.

## Cash as a reputational asset

The most interesting lesson from Conviviality is that cash is not only a financial asset. It is also a reputational asset, and in some sectors it is vital. When a company depends on suppliers, credit insurers, banks and recurring customers, that perception matters enormously. It is almost the greatest asset after a well-run operation.

In distribution, trust is part of the balance sheet.

If a supplier believes you will pay, it gives you terms.  
If an insurer believes you are reliable, it covers the exposure.  
If a bank believes you understand your cash, it maintains credit lines.  
If an investor believes the problem is contained, it can provide capital.

## The mistake was not only financial

It is tempting to read the case as an accounting problem: an unforeseen *tax bill*, a poor forecast, an urgent financing need.

But the lesson is deeper.

The mistake was not only not having the money. It was failing to understand that cash was the nervous system of the business, not trust, dominance or market positioning. Those were consequences of the former.

In distribution, retail, consumer goods or any operation intensive in working capital and narrow margins, profitability without liquidity can be a dangerous illusion.

## The strategic lesson

Conviviality teaches an uncomfortable lesson for any operating company:

**cash is not a secondary result of strategy; it is a condition of existence**.

If the liquidity system breaks, time compresses.

Decisions that could once be made in months must be made in days.  
Negotiations that were once commercial become defensive.  
Suppliers who once wanted to sell now want to collect.  
Investors who once looked at growth now look at survival.

When that happens, the company loses its most important asset: **control of the calendar**. The Conviviality case does not show a company without a business. It shows something more dangerous: a company with a great business, but without oxygen.

There is an illusion that a business can be run without cash. Overleverage or scaling too quickly are, in most cases, the cause. But Conviviality is more raw and direct, because it was purely and simply this: **a forecasting error**.
